A Bestiary of Vanishings

What Grows in the Dark Remembers Everything

Andrew Thomas Huang and Stephanie Zhang, with Sebastien Zany (technical direction, programming, simulations), kk.Shucko (sound) and Siddharth Doshi (materials science). 2026, ongoing.

The Shen is a mollusk deity from Chinese myth whose breath makes mirages. A Bestiary of Vanishings (in collaboration with Andrew Thomas Huang) is a mythopoetic sci-fi art game in which players uncover the memoirs of the Shen through feeding a far-future slime mold intelligence. It is also a series of bioprinted cuttlefish whose vasculature is printed in a living slime mold ink, building toward a two-foot Shen that will be printed live at Gray Area, San Francisco.

Bioprinted in the Skylar-Scott Lab, Stanford, with vessels designed in svVascularize (Sexton et al., Science, 2025) and a photonic skin from Doshi et al. (Nature, 2026).

The prints

I draw each cuttlefish with a needle directly inside a block of clear carbopol gel, which holds every printed line in place as the body and its vasculature are built up (embedded 3D bioprinting). Under ultraviolet light the body glows blue and the Physarum vasculature glows green or orange, depending on the ink.

In visible light the newest body ink, semi-transparent and loaded with mica powder, shimmers as the print turns. The white print is made with it. Earlier prints used a pale blue body ink. Prints from 2026.

Bioprinters

The bioprinters are built in the lab from aluminium gantries that move a needle in three axes, towers of syringe pumps that push ink through it, and a motion controller that runs each toolpath as G-code. They print custom living bioinks (see Ink development, below). The largest, made for Gray Area, is a gantry over a 65-litre glass tank holding 64 litres of carbopol, mixed in four-litre batches over eight days. During the exhibition it will print a two-foot Shen live. Also shown: frames, pumps, the controller, a linear rail, an earlier custom bioprinter and bioreactor printing a heart (video by Yi Yi Du), and printed hearts in bioreactors.

Vascularizing the cuttlefish

The vessels are designed in software, where svVascularize (Sexton et al.) grows a branching vascular tree inside the cuttlefish mesh and out into its curled tentacles. We then prune the tree down to what the printer can draw while keeping every part of the body close to a vessel, convert it into a collision-free toolpath with X-CAVATE, and print it with two nozzles in a single bath. The lower body goes down first in blue ink, then the orange vessel network, and then the upper body closes over it. The orange-veined print shown above under UV, about 120 mm long, is the result.

Figures from Zhang, Solberg and Skylar-Scott, “Model-guided vascularization of arbitrary, non-anatomical geometries by dual-nozzle embedded 3D bioprinting,” research poster, Stanford University, 2026. svVascularize is from Sexton, Rütsche et al., Science 388, 1198-1204 (2025).

Ink development

The vasculature is printed in a fluorescent bioink made from Physarum polycephalum, a single-celled slime mold intelligence that builds networks as it forages, while the body is printed in a separate, semi-transparent ink.

Simulations

The game’s three simulations are by Sebastien Zany, rendering the deity’s iridescent skin from color measured on real photonic films, modeling the thin-film optics of those films, and running a slime mold of about 250,000 agents.

Emergent Vessels

2025, ongoing. Skylar-Scott Lab, Stanford.

Engineered organs are limited by their blood supply. Cells more than a few hundred micrometres from a vessel die, so a printed organ needs a network of vessels that fluid can flow through. Embedded 3D bioprinting draws that network with a needle inside a granular gel bath, which holds every printed line in place until the surrounding tissue sets.

Emergent Vessels turns this method toward living organisms, 3D bioprinting self-assembling, living sculptures via novel bioinks of Neurospora crassa fungi and Physarum polycephalum slime mold. Over a few days the organisms colonize the printed form and grow it into capillary-like networks.

Based on Sexton, Rütsche et al., “Rapid model-guided design of organ-scale synthetic vasculature for biomanufacturing,” Science 388, 1198-1204 (2025) (Skylar-Scott and Marsden Labs, Stanford).

A printed network turning in visible light, printing fluorescent networks into gel cubes under UV light, the finished networks, the installation at Gray Area Festival, San Francisco, 2025, and Printessa, a bioprinter printing live at the festival (photos: Barak Shrama).

Virtual vessels

The vessel networks are first designed in software, where svVascularize (Sexton et al.) grows branching vascular trees to fill a shape and simulates blood flow through them, shaded here by pressure. It runs about 230 times faster than earlier methods and can design whole organs with a million vessels. Each design is then printed into a support bath, shown here in front and side views at 900× speed, where the printed structures self-assemble into vascular networks.

All three from Sexton, Rütsche et al., Science 388, 1198-1204 (2025).

Synthetic Cesia

2025, ongoing. In collaboration with Siddharth Doshi.

Dynamic sculptures made with cuttlefish-inspired photonic skins that change color and texture. Cuttlefish shift color in moments, partly through iridophores, stacks of thin plates in the skin that color it by interference rather than pigment. These soft photonic skins work the same way. Each is a Fabry-Pérot cavity, a film of the conducting polymer PEDOT:PSS thinner than a wavelength of light, held between two layers of gold. Light reflects back and forth between the gold layers and only the wavelengths that fit the gap reinforce one another, so the thickness of the film sets its color. It is the same structural color found in a soap bubble or a beetle’s shell.

When the polymer takes up water it swells, widening the gap and moving the color through the spectrum. Patterning the film with an electron beam changes how much each region can swell, so spots and camouflage patterns emerge from an otherwise uniform film. In the video above, a skin’s spots merge into one color as it swells (video: Siddharth Doshi).

Based on Doshi et al., “Soft photonic skins with dynamic texture and colour control,” Nature 649, 345-352 (2026) (Melosh and Brongersma Labs, Stanford).

Making the skins

The skins are made in a cleanroom, where a gold mirror is laid down first, the polymer is spin-coated over it (the spin speed sets the film’s thickness, and so its color), and a thin layer of gold goes on top. The gold is deposited in an electron-beam evaporator, a vacuum chamber in which an electron beam heats the metal until it vaporizes and condenses onto the substrates. Each film is then patterned and tested.

New skins are now being made from silk, patterned with thermal stamps and beads for iridescence, so they can scale into flexible, camouflaging textiles for sculpture.

Research

The works are made inside research labs at Stanford and build on two papers, Sexton et al. (Science, 2025) and Doshi et al. (Nature, 2026). The vascular design of the bioprinted cuttlefish is documented in our research poster.
